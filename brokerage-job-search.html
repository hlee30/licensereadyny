How I Looked for a Real Estate Brokerage After Taking the NY Salesperson Exam

Taking the real estate course and passing the exam is only part of getting started. I quickly learned that choosing the right brokerage can be just as important — especially if you are new, working part-time, and still learning how actual transactions work.

My Situation

When I finished my coursework through NYREI, I was not looking for a traditional full-time real estate position. I wanted to build a residential real estate business part-time while continuing my primary career in advertising and analytics.

I also referee soccer, which means some evenings and weekends can already be committed. Because of that, I knew from the beginning that flexibility had to be one of my biggest criteria.

I was particularly interested in residential sales, working with buyers and eventually sellers, rather than building a business primarily around apartment rentals.

1. Start With Your Real Estate School's Placement Resources

One of the first things I did after completing NYREI was look into its job-placement resources and brokerage relationships.

This was useful because I didn't have to start completely from scratch. Real estate schools regularly interact with brokerages that are already accustomed to recruiting newly licensed salespeople.

My tip: Don't just tell the placement office that you are "looking for a brokerage." Be specific about what you need.

My criteria were closer to:

  • Newly licensed agents accepted
  • Part-time agents accepted
  • Independent-contractor arrangement
  • Training and mentorship
  • Opportunities to work with buyers and sellers
  • Hands-on help with my first transactions
  • Flexibility around another full-time job

2. Decide What You Actually Need From Your First Brokerage

At first, it is easy to focus almost entirely on commission split. I eventually realized that the highest advertised split isn't necessarily the best deal for a brand-new salesperson.

I began evaluating brokerages based on what would actually help me complete my first few transactions.

Questions I started asking

  • Who helps me write my first offer?
  • Who reviews my paperwork?
  • Can I call someone when I have a question during a transaction?
  • Is there one-on-one mentorship?
  • Does the brokerage provide buyer or seller leads?
  • How are company-generated leads distributed?
  • What happens if I bring in my own client?
  • Are there transaction, desk, technology, or monthly fees?
  • Is there a production minimum?
  • What happens when I am unavailable because of my primary job or another commitment?

3. Look Beyond Traditional Brokerages

One of the biggest things I discovered during my search was that I did not have to limit myself to the traditional brokerage model.

I explored both traditional brokerages and firms using discount or commission-rebate models.

Some of the companies I researched or spoke with included NextStopNY, NestApple, Hauseit, and Mont Sky/HomeDax.

The experience made me realize that the business model matters because it can affect where leads come from, what kind of clients you work with, how much support you receive, and how your commission is structured.

My tip: Search for the brokerage model that fits the way you plan to work, rather than automatically applying only to the biggest brokerage names.

4. Compare Company Leads and Self-Generated Business Separately

One question that became particularly important was whether the economics changed depending on who generated the client.

During my research, I encountered brokerage models where company-generated leads and self-generated clients could have very different commission arrangements.

That distinction matters.

A brokerage may advertise an attractive commission split, but you need to understand whether that applies to:

  • Clients you generate yourself
  • Leads provided by the brokerage
  • Both
  • Only after you reach a certain production level
Ask directly: "What is my commission split on a company-generated client, and what is my split if I bring in the client myself?"

5. Ask How You Will Get Your First Three Transactions

This became one of my favorite ways to evaluate a brokerage.

Instead of asking only about general training, I started thinking about a much more practical question:

If I join your brokerage tomorrow, how would you help me get through my first three transactions?

The answer can tell you much more than a generic promise of "training."

I wanted to understand whether training meant prerecorded videos and occasional meetings, or whether someone would actually guide me through offers, negotiations, paperwork, client communication, and closing.

6. Be Upfront About Being Part-Time

I did not try to hide the fact that I had another career. I explained that I was available primarily during weekday evenings and weekends and that soccer refereeing could occasionally affect weekend availability.

That helped me eliminate brokerages where the expectations would clearly conflict with my schedule.

It also helped me identify firms that were genuinely comfortable working with part-time independent contractors, rather than firms that technically accepted part-time agents but expected full-time availability.

My tip: Ask what "part-time friendly" actually means before joining.

7. Ask About Showing Coverage

Availability matters in residential real estate. Buyers may want to see a property while you are at your primary job, traveling, or handling another commitment.

That made showing coverage an important question for me.

I wanted to know whether another agent could help with a showing when necessary and what that arrangement would cost or how compensation would work.

For someone pursuing real estate part-time, this can be just as important as commission split.

8. Don't Ignore Monthly and Transaction Fees

A commission percentage does not tell you what you will actually keep.

I learned to ask about the entire cost structure:

  • Monthly fees
  • Desk fees
  • Technology fees
  • Transaction fees
  • Marketing costs
  • Lead-related costs
  • Any required memberships or systems

This is especially important for a part-time salesperson. If you close only a few transactions initially, recurring monthly expenses can make an otherwise attractive commission split much less attractive.

9. Treat Brokerage Interviews as Two-Way Interviews

One thing that became obvious during conversations with brokerages was that I was interviewing them as much as they were interviewing me.

I wanted to know:

  • Who would supervise me?
  • How accessible is that person?
  • What kind of clients would I realistically work with?
  • How would leads actually reach me?
  • How are new agents taught the transaction process?
  • What are the expectations for responsiveness?
  • Can the model realistically work around my schedule?

10. Follow Up

I contacted multiple brokerages by email, completed applications, scheduled calls, and followed up when I did not immediately receive a response.

Some conversations moved from email to phone calls or meetings. In at least one case, scheduling and travel from Brooklyn made a phone conversation more practical than an office meeting.

The process reminded me that brokerage recruiting is still a job search. Professional follow-up matters.

The Brokerage Comparison Framework I Would Use Today

Question Why I Care
Do you accept part-time agents? My real estate work has to coexist with my primary career.
Who mentors me? I want hands-on help with early transactions.
Do you provide leads? Company opportunities can help a new agent gain experience.
What is the company-lead split? It may differ substantially from self-generated business.
What is the self-generated split? I want to understand the long-term economics.
What recurring fees will I pay? Fixed costs matter more when starting part-time.
Is showing coverage available? I cannot always leave my primary job for a showing.
Is there a production minimum? I need realistic expectations while building my business.
How will I get through my first three deals? This tells me whether the training is really hands-on.

My Biggest Takeaway

When you are newly licensed, I would not choose a brokerage based only on the highest commission percentage.

My priority became finding a brokerage where I could actually learn how to complete transactions, get access to opportunities, ask questions, and build enough experience to eventually generate my own book of business.

A slightly less favorable split can potentially be worth it at the beginning if the brokerage genuinely helps you learn the business.
