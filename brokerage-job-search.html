AFTER THE EXAM

How I Looked for a Real Estate Brokerage After Taking the NY Salesperson Exam

Passing the exam is one step. Figuring out which brokerage to join is a completely different decision.

My Situation

When I started looking for a brokerage, I wasn't trying to immediately turn real estate into my only career. I wanted to enter residential sales, learn how actual transactions work, and start building experience while continuing my primary career.

That made my brokerage search a little different. I wasn't simply asking which company had the biggest name. I needed to understand which brokerage would actually work for someone entering the industry part-time.

Start With Your Real Estate School

One of the first places I looked was the job-placement side of the real estate school where I completed my coursework.

Schools often already have relationships with brokerages that regularly recruit newly licensed salespeople.

What I would tell the placement office:

I'm looking for an NYC brokerage that accepts newly licensed agents working part-time as independent contractors. I'm especially interested in strong training, mentorship, and opportunities to work rentals or buyer leads while I build my own book of business.

Figure Out What You Actually Need From Your First Brokerage

Before talking to brokerages, I found it useful to separate what sounded impressive from what would actually help me close my first few transactions.

  • Who answers questions when I'm working on my first deal?
  • Is there actual training after I join?
  • Will someone review contracts and paperwork with me?
  • Are there company leads?
  • What fees do I owe even if I don't close anything?
  • Can another agent cover a showing if I'm unavailable?

Don't Assume You Have to Join a Traditional Brokerage

One thing I learned quickly was that brokerages don't all operate the same way.

I looked at traditional brokerages, but I also researched firms with lower-overhead or rebate-oriented models. I spoke with or researched companies including NextStopNY, NestApple, Hauseit, and Mont Sky/HomeDax.

Those conversations helped me see that the real question wasn't necessarily traditional versus nontraditional. It was whether the brokerage's structure matched the way I planned to build my business.

Ask About Company Leads vs. Your Own Leads

A brokerage saying that it provides leads sounds great, but I learned that it's worth asking exactly what that means.

I wanted to know whether those were real opportunities regularly distributed to new agents, internet inquiries, rental leads, buyer inquiries, or simply occasional opportunities.

A useful question: If I join tomorrow without an existing client, what opportunities would I realistically have during my first 90 days?

Ask How They'll Help You Get Through Your First Three Deals

Training sounds good in a recruiting presentation. What mattered more to me was what would happen when I had an actual client.

Who would review the paperwork? Who could I call if something unexpected happened? Would another agent or broker walk me through the process?

That's where mentorship started to matter more to me than generic training videos.

Be Honest About Being Part-Time

I didn't want to join a brokerage and then surprise them with my availability.

I was upfront that I was looking to build my real estate business around another primary career.

I would rather know before joining that a company expects agents to be available throughout every weekday than discover that afterward.

Ask What Happens If You Can't Make a Showing

This became one of my most practical questions.

Real estate clients aren't always going to request showings at times that perfectly fit my schedule.

Could another agent show the property? Would I pay that agent a showing fee? Would the brokerage help coordinate it?

Pay Attention to All the Fees

Commission splits are only one piece of brokerage economics.

  • Monthly desk or technology fees
  • Transaction fees
  • Errors and omissions insurance
  • MLS or listing-related costs
  • Marketing expenses
  • Mentorship fees or additional splits
  • Minimum production requirements

Remember That You're Interviewing Them Too

When you're newly licensed, it can feel like the entire conversation is about whether the brokerage will accept you.

But choosing a sponsoring broker is also a business decision on your side.

Follow Up

After conversations, I found it helpful to send a short follow-up email and keep notes about each brokerage.

Once you've spoken to several companies, the details start blending together surprisingly quickly.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

The Questions I'd Ask a Brokerage

Topic Question
Training What does training look like during my first 90 days?
Mentorship Who helps me through my first few transactions?
Leads Does the company provide leads to new agents?
Availability Do you work with agents who have another full-time career?
Showings Is showing coverage available if I can't attend?
Costs What fixed, monthly, transaction, and other fees should I expect?
Production Is there a minimum production requirement?

MY BIGGEST TAKEAWAY

Don't Choose a Brokerage Based on the Name Alone

For my first brokerage, I care much more about what will happen after I join than how recognizable the logo is.

The right brokerage for one new salesperson may be completely wrong for another.

REAL ESTATE IN REAL LIFE

What My Cold Plunge Taught Me About Real Estate

Real estate school taught me the terminology. A cold plunge on my condo balcony taught me why any of it actually matters.

Real Estate School Felt Academic—Until It Didn't

When I was studying for the New York real estate salesperson exam, a lot of the material initially felt academic.

Agency. Contracts. Property rights. Condominiums. Offering plans. Building rules. Fiduciary duties.

I understood why I needed to learn them for the exam. What I didn't expect was how quickly some of those concepts would become relevant to my own home.

It Started With a Cold Plunge

I have a portable cold plunge on the balcony of my Brooklyn condo.

Building management raised concerns about the weight and safety of the plunge and asked that it be drained while the issue was reviewed.

Suddenly I wasn't studying abstract property concepts anymore.

What started as:

“Can I put this cold plunge on my balcony?”

turned into questions about engineering, condominium governance, building documents, insurance, structural loads, and owner rights.

I Started Reading the Documents I Had Barely Looked At Before

Like a lot of condo owners, I had received a large collection of documents when I bought my apartment.

But there is a major difference between possessing those documents and actually understanding them.

  • The condominium by-laws
  • The offering plan
  • House rules
  • Board provisions
  • Owner restrictions
  • Alteration requirements

The First Question Became: Where Is the Rule?

One of the most useful habits I've developed from studying real estate is asking where a requirement actually comes from.

  • The by-laws?
  • The offering plan?
  • A house rule?
  • A board policy?
  • An alteration agreement?
  • A property manager's interpretation?

Those aren't necessarily the same thing.

Owning a Condo Is More Complicated Than Owning Four Walls

Condo ownership isn't simply ownership of the interior of an apartment.

There can be unit property, common elements, and limited common elements, with different rights and responsibilities attached to each.

  • Board powers
  • Owner obligations
  • Insurance
  • Maintenance
  • Voting rights
  • Alterations
  • Use restrictions

Then Came the Math

My cold plunge holds approximately 80 gallons of water.

Water weighs roughly 8.34 pounds per gallon.

80 gallons × 8.34 lbs = about 667 lbs of water

And that's before considering the tub itself or the person sitting inside it.

The footprint is approximately 6.25 square feet, which means the issue quickly became one of load distribution and structural capacity.

Real Estate and Engineering Suddenly Overlapped

  • Live loads
  • Distributed loads
  • Structural drawings
  • Engineering opinions
  • Alteration agreements
  • Liability and insurance

The Process I Started Using

01

What document governs this?

Find the actual source of the rule.

02

Who has authority?

Determine who can actually make or enforce the decision.

03

Is this a rule or an interpretation?

Separate written requirements from someone's interpretation of them.

04

What evidence resolves the question?

Sometimes that means a document. Sometimes it means an engineer. Sometimes it means both.

Real Estate Touches Almost Everything

Mortgages Interest rates Property taxes Insurance Construction Zoning Rent Equity Inheritance Local government Banking Consumer spending

It Changed How I Would Buy My Next Condo

  • The by-laws
  • Alteration rules
  • Board authority
  • Limited common elements
  • Insurance requirements
  • Pet and leasing restrictions
  • Election procedures
  • Building financials
  • Capital projects
  • Recurring expenses

MY BIGGEST TAKEAWAY

Read the Boring Documents Before They Become Interesting

Offering plans and by-laws aren't particularly exciting when everything is going smoothly.

They become much more interesting the moment there's a disagreement about what you can or cannot do with your own property.

Real estate school didn't give me the answer to my cold-plunge situation.

It gave me a better way to ask the questions.

Sometimes it just takes an 80-gallon tub of cold water to make you notice it.
