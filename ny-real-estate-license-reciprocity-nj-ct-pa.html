NY LICENSE RECIPROCITY

Can I Use My New York Real Estate License in NJ, Connecticut or Pennsylvania?

I started with a New York real estate salesperson license, then decided I wanted the option to work across state lines too. I’m now almost done with New Jersey’s 75-hour prelicensing course — and this is what I wish I knew before I started.

Reviewed October 15, 2026 · Always verify current rules with the state licensing agency.

WHAT I WISH I KNEW

No reciprocity does not always mean “start completely over.”

New York currently has no real estate license reciprocity with any state. But that does not mean your New York license and education are useless everywhere else. New Jersey has an education-waiver route, Connecticut has a non-reciprocal application route, and Pennsylvania can give credit for qualifying out-of-state education and licensing history.

First: what does real estate license reciprocity actually mean?

“Reciprocity” sounds like one of those licensing words that should be simple, but somehow becomes confusing the second you start Googling it.

In plain English, reciprocity is when one state recognizes a license from another state under a formal agreement and gives that licensee a streamlined path to become licensed there.

It does not necessarily mean you can drive across the state line with your New York salesperson license and immediately start showing houses. You still need to satisfy whatever rules the second state requires.

And this is where I got tripped up: I initially treated “no reciprocity” as if it meant “none of my New York work counts.” Those are not the same thing.

New York’s current status: no reciprocity with any state

The New York Department of State currently says that it does not have reciprocity with any other state. So there is no automatic license swap where you hand another state your NY license and walk away with theirs.

New York itself also has a waiver process for applicants who completed qualifying education outside New York. That is a good example of why “reciprocity” and “credit for what you already did” are two different questions.

NEW JERSEY

No reciprocity — but there is an education-waiver path

New Jersey is the state that made me wish I had researched this earlier. NJ is very clear that it does not have reciprocity with any state. The normal salesperson path is a 75-hour New Jersey prelicensing course, the licensing exam, and an application through a sponsoring broker.

I’ll admit it: coming from New York, I kept thinking “77-hour course.” New York is 77 hours. New Jersey is 75 hours. I’m almost done with that NJ course now.

Here’s the part I wish I had understood before enrolling: New Jersey has a salesperson education-waiver process for people who were licensed in another state.

NJ’s current waiver criteria say an out-of-state salesperson may qualify if they were actively licensed within the past three years, completed a state-approved prelicensing course, and qualified by examination. If the original education totaled at least 75 hours, the waiver form says no additional coursework documentation is required beyond the required license certification.

New Jersey even publishes a separate guide specifically for New York salesperson licensees. For people licensed under New York’s newer education requirements, that waiver route is absolutely something I would investigate before automatically signing up for another full course.

Important: a waiver is not a New Jersey license. Approval of the education waiver does not eliminate the NJ licensing exam or the rest of the licensing process.

MY TAKEAWAY

Ask about the waiver before paying for 75 more hours.

If I were starting over, I would submit the waiver paperwork first, wait for New Jersey’s determination, and only enroll in additional education if I actually needed it.

CONNECTICUT

New York applicants now use Connecticut’s non-reciprocity route

Connecticut is another place where old information online can be misleading. Connecticut says the New York Department of State terminated the reciprocal agreement, so New York applicants now apply through Non-Reciprocity.

That sounds like you are starting from zero, but the current Connecticut process is more nuanced than that.

For the non-reciprocal path, Connecticut currently requires an active out-of-state real estate license in good standing, proof that you passed a written licensing examination in the state where you are actively licensed, license-history documentation, and successful completion of the Connecticut state portion of the licensing examination.

So again: no reciprocity, but also not necessarily “repeat every single thing.”

PENNSYLVANIA

No NY reciprocity anymore, but your NY coursework can still matter

Pennsylvania is probably the state where I would be most careful about old reciprocity lists. Pennsylvania specifically says it no longer issues new reciprocal licenses based on an active New York license.

Pennsylvania’s current reciprocity agreements are with Arkansas, Georgia, Louisiana, Maryland and Massachusetts — not New York.

But a New York licensee can still use Pennsylvania’s standard method, and this is where the existing NY work can help.

Pennsylvania’s current licensing guide says its 75-hour education requirement may be satisfied by 75 hours of prelicensing coursework completed in another state or jurisdiction if that coursework was approved to meet licensing requirements there.

It also says that someone who held an active salesperson license in another state within the last five years does not need to take the General/National portion of the Pennsylvania examination. A currently licensed out-of-state applicant uses the Pennsylvania state portion after meeting the education requirement.

You also need an employing Pennsylvania broker before applying for the salesperson license.

The quick version

State Full reciprocity with NY? What your NY background may do for you
New Jersey No Possible education waiver instead of repeating the full 75-hour NJ course, if you meet the waiver criteria.
Connecticut No Non-reciprocal application; qualifying active out-of-state licensees take the Connecticut state portion of the exam.
Pennsylvania No Qualifying out-of-state prelicensing education can meet the 75-hour requirement, and recent active out-of-state licensees can avoid the General/National exam portion.

The big lesson: “No reciprocity” and “start completely over” are not the same thing.

What I would do differently

  1. Start with the official state licensing website. I would not make a decision based on an old school blog or a reciprocity list that may not have been updated.
  2. Search for “waiver” and “non-reciprocal,” not just “reciprocity.” That one vocabulary change can completely change what you find.
  3. Get the state’s determination before enrolling in another course. If a waiver might apply, I would rather know that first than find out when I’m almost finished with another 75 hours.
  4. Never assume neighboring states work the same way. New Jersey, Connecticut and Pennsylvania are all right next to New York, and they still handle a NY license three different ways.

Is getting licensed in multiple states worth it?

For me, the appeal is flexibility. I started in New York, but New Jersey is close enough that being able to work legally on both sides of the river gives me more options. Once I started going through the NJ process, Connecticut and Pennsylvania naturally became the next question.

If you are in the same position, I would not assume your New York license automatically travels with you — but I also would not assume you need to throw away everything you already completed.

Check the current rules first. I learned that a little later than I would have liked. Hopefully you don’t have to.
